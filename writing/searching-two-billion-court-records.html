Searching two billion court records, and the ones that weren't there

Making the search fast was an indexing problem. Making it correct was not — the records our users needed most were the ones the courts had never written down.

Elasticsearch· Python· Legal-tech, India· 8 min read

Contents

  2. The first search is the expensive one
  3. The documents that lied by omission
  4. The thing I would look at again

Every working day, courts across India publish a causelist: the set of matters that will be heard in front of a particular bench, on a particular day. Every court publishes its own. Some publish clean HTML. Some publish a PDF that is really a scan of a table. Some publish for tomorrow, some for the next three weeks, some whenever they feel like it.

We were fetching all of them, on a schedule, and turning them into something a lawyer could subscribe to. If your matter was listed, you got told. If a keyword you cared about showed up anywhere in the country, you got told.

Two parts of that turned out to be much harder than they looked. The first was making the initial search fast. The second was finding cases that the causelists did not actually mention.

The short version

  • ~2 billion text blobs, one per parsed matter, in Elasticsearch — deliberately flat text, because every attempt at a strict schema broke on some registry's formatting.
  • The expensive query was not the recurring alert. It was the first one: a new subscription backfilling its entire history while the user waits.
  • Time-bucketed indices turned "search two billion" into "search the handful of buckets that overlap the date range", and the slices then ran in parallel.
  • None of that fixed recall. Causelists truncate multi-party matters to & ORS, so the party our user was paying us to find was never in the document.
  • The fix was to detect the truncation marker and go fetch the full party list from the court's per-case page — a second blob indexed beside the first.

01 What we were searching

For every matter we parsed, we stored a blob: a flat piece of text holding everything we had extracted for that case. Party names, advocates, the case type, number and year. Mongo held the blobs as the source of truth, and they were synced into Elasticsearch almost immediately after being written.

A blob rather than a neat schema, because we were matching arbitrary user keywords against text whose shape changed from court to court. Every time we tried to force the parsed output into structured fields, some registry's formatting convention broke the assumption. Flat searchable text was the thing that held up.

Across every court and every day we had been collecting, that came to roughly two billion blobs.

02 The first search is the expensive one

The alerting query is small. It runs every twenty to thirty minutes, looks back over the last forty-five, and only ever touches matters that arrived in that window. It is a narrow query over a tiny slice of recent data, and it is not the problem.

The problem is what happens the moment a user subscribes.

When a bank adds a keyword, they do not only want to know about matters listed tomorrow. They want the history. Everything we have ever indexed that matches. That single action kicks off a search across the entire corpus — not a window, not a slice, all two billion blobs — and the user is sitting there waiting for it.

The first run of every subscription was the worst query in the system, and it happened at the worst possible moment: the first thirty seconds someone spends with the product.

Not every mention is the same mention

The first thing we did was stop treating the blob as one undifferentiated lump.

If a bank subscribes to its own name, that name can appear in a matter in several different roles. They might have filed the case. The case might have been filed against them. Or they might just be referenced somewhere in the text of a matter that has nothing to do with them directly.

Those are three quite different pieces of news, and in-house legal teams usually care most about the second one. So alongside the blob, we extracted and stored the petitioner and respondent names as their own fields on the Elasticsearch document.

That let a subscription be more specific than a keyword. Track everything mentioning us. Or track only matters where we are the respondent. A search that had been one query over one text field became a combined query: match the term in the petitioner field, or the respondent field, or the body of the blob, depending on what the user had actually asked for.

It also helped performance, because a query restricted to party fields is matching against far less text than one scanning the full blob.

Time is the only thing every court agrees on

The real win came from how we laid the data out.

Court records have a property that makes them easier than they first appear: they are naturally ordered in time, and almost every query against them is bounded in time. A causelist belongs to a date. An alert cares about the last hour. Even a historical backfill usually has a sensible floor — nobody is looking for keyword matches from fifteen years ago on a subscription they created this morning.

So instead of one enormous index, we wrote blobs into time-bucketed indices, split by day and by month. A query with a date range no longer had to consider the whole corpus. It only had to touch the indices whose buckets overlapped that range, and Elasticsearch could skip everything else without reading it.

This is what turned the number from two billion into something manageable. In practice, a search bounded to the last two years is a search over a defined, countable set of small indices, and the ones outside that range cost nothing.

It also meant the incremental alerting query — the one that runs every twenty minutes, forever — only ever touched the newest index. The hot path stopped competing with the cold data entirely.

Running the slices in parallel

Once the data was partitioned by time, the backfill stopped being one large query and became many small independent ones. Each time slice could be searched on its own, and none of them depended on the results of any other.

So we ran them in parallel and merged the results, rather than issuing one query and waiting for the cluster to grind through everything sequentially. The user's wait time went from the sum of all the slices to roughly the slowest one.

The trade-off

Finer time buckets prune better, but more indices means more shards, and shard count is not free — the cluster pays for every one of them in memory and coordination overhead. Daily granularity for recent data and coarser buckets for older data was the compromise we landed on, because recent data is what gets queried with precision and old data mostly gets swept in bulk.

03 The documents that lied by omission

The search worked. And it still missed cases.

Here is why. Indian causelists are written for people standing in a courtroom, not for machines. When a matter has multiple parties on one side, the list does not print all of them. It prints the first one and then an abbreviation — & ANR, & ORS, or some local variant — meaning "and another" or "and others."

So a matter with a bank as the fourth respondent appears in the causelist as Sharma vs State & Ors. The bank's name is simply not in the document.

No amount of tuning fixes this. Better relevance, better analyzers, better tokenization, a bigger cluster — none of it matters, because the string you are searching for was never written down.

And these are not edge cases. Multi-party matters are extremely common, and the consequence of missing one is not a slightly worse search result. It is a client not knowing they are listed.

Detecting an absence

What we could detect was the marker itself. If a parsed matter contained one of those truncation tokens, we knew two things: that the party list was incomplete, and — because we had already extracted the case type, number and year — exactly which case it was.

Most courts expose a per-case detail page that lists every party properly. So for any matter flagged as truncated, we went and fetched it.

The parties from that page became a second, supplementary blob, stored against the same case and indexed alongside the original. The causelist blob recorded what was listed; the supplementary blob recorded who was actually involved. A keyword search hitting either one was a match.

That closed the gap. A bank buried four names deep in a respondent list now surfaced, even though the causelist that triggered the alert never named them.

What it cost

It is not free. Every truncated matter becomes an extra fetch against a court website that is usually slow, frequently unavailable, and generally not built with our traffic in mind. On a heavy listing day that is a lot of additional requests, and they queue behind the same bot detection and scheduling constraints as everything else.

A false positive is mild annoyance. A false negative is a client missing a hearing. When the cost of the two errors is that lopsided, you spend on recall and you stop agonising over it.

04 The thing I would look at again

The alerting design is where I would start.

We polled. Every twenty to thirty minutes, look back over the last forty-five, find new matters, check them against the subscription list, send what matched. The window deliberately overlapped the interval so that nothing could slip through a boundary, and deduplication handled the repeats. At-least-once, with a cheap fix for the duplicates, rather than trying to build exactly-once and getting it subtly wrong.

That is sound, but it is search running backwards. Normal search is one query against many documents. Alerting is many standing queries against each arriving document, and Elasticsearch has a feature built for exactly that shape — the percolator, which stores the queries and matches documents against them as they land.

Whether it would have held up at our subscription count, I genuinely do not know. Every percolator query lives in memory, and "thousands of keyword subscriptions" is a different scale of problem from the examples in the documentation. But we never seriously evaluated it, and the reason we never evaluated it was that polling already worked. That is a reasonable engineering answer and also the way systems quietly ossify.

If I built this again, I would at least find out.
